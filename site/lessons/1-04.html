    NSE, BSE and SEBI: who does what

    Two exchanges run the marketplace, a few behind-the-scenes institutions make sure every trade settles, and one regulator writes the rules for everyone. Knowing who does what tells you who protects your money and where to complain when something goes wrong.

    About 45 minutesReal cases: 1992 scam, Karvy 2019, Adani 2023, SEBI's F&O study
    Part 1

    The idea in plain words

    Think of a big, well-run vegetable mandi. There is the market yard where buyers and sellers meet (the exchange), the licensed commission agents who bring you in (the brokers), a cashier's office that makes sure money and goods actually change hands (the clearing corporation), a cold store where goods are kept in your name (the depository), and a market committee that sets the rules and punishes cheats (the regulator). The stock market has exactly these roles.

    RoleWho in IndiaWhat they do for you
    RegulatorSEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India), Mumbai, set up by law in 1992Writes the rules, registers brokers and advisers, investigates fraud, can fine and ban
    ExchangesNSE (National Stock Exchange, trading since 1994) and BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange, founded 1875, Asia's oldest)Run the electronic market where orders are matched; list companies; publish prices; watch for manipulation
    Clearing corporationsNSE Clearing (NCL) and Indian Clearing Corporation (ICCL, for BSE)Stand in the middle of every trade, so you never depend on the other trader paying up
    DepositoriesNSDL and CDSLHold your shares electronically, in your name, in your demat account
    BrokersZerodha, Groww, Upstox, Angel One and others, all SEBI-registeredTake your orders to the exchange; you can't trade directly on NSE

    NSE vs BSE

    Most large companies are listed on both. You can buy Reliance on NSE and sell it on BSE. The prices stay within a few paise to a rupee of each other, because traders instantly buy on the cheaper exchange and sell on the dearer one. NSE handles the large majority of cash-market trading by value, so prices there are usually the reference. The Nifty 50 is NSE's index and the Sensex is BSE's (next lesson).

    How SEBI protects you

    • Registration: every broker, depository participant, mutual fund, portfolio manager, investment adviser (RIA) and research analyst (RA) must be registered. You can check any of them on sebi.gov.in.
    • Your money and shares are kept separate: brokers must keep client money and securities apart from their own, and send unused client money back to your bank regularly.
    • Disclosure: listed companies must publish results every quarter and announce price-sensitive news to the exchanges immediately.
    • Insider trading and manipulation are crimes: trading on unpublished information, or "pump and dump" schemes, can lead to bans, fines and prosecution.
    • A place to complain: SEBI's SCORES portal, plus the exchanges' investor grievance cells and the online dispute resolution (ODR) system. Exchanges also run Investor Protection Funds for when a broker defaults.
    Watch out

    Anyone on YouTube, Telegram or Instagram who gives you specific buy or sell calls for money must be SEBI-registered as an RIA or RA. Since 2024 SEBI has barred registered firms from working with unregistered "finfluencers". If they can't show a SEBI registration number, ignore them.

    Part 2

    Real cases that shaped the rules

    1992: The Harshad Mehta scam and the birth of NSE

    In 1992 a broker named Harshad Mehta used fake bank receipts to pour borrowed money into shares, pushing the Sensex up about 4 times in a year before it collapsed. Trading then was on a noisy floor with paper certificates and settlement that could take weeks. The fallout gave SEBI legal powers (the SEBI Act, 1992) and led to NSE, which launched fully electronic, screen-based trading in 1994. NSDL followed in 1996 so shares could be held electronically instead of on paper.

    2019: Karvy and your shares in someone else's hands

    In November 2019 SEBI found that Karvy Stock Broking had used a power of attorney from its clients to pledge their shares, worth about ₹2,000 crore, to raise loans for itself. SEBI and the exchanges stepped in, and most clients got their shares back. The rules were then tightened: brokers can no longer move your shares through a blanket power of attorney, and any pledge of your shares now needs your own approval by OTP. This is why your broker asks you to authorise each sale with a CDSL or NSDL OTP (TPIN).

    SEBI's own numbers on F&O traders

    SEBI studies what happens to retail traders. Its study published in September 2024 found that about 93% of individual traders in equity futures and options lost money between FY22 and FY24, with total losses of about ₹1.8 lakh crore. The average loss was around ₹2 lakh per person. SEBI tightened F&O rules from late 2024 because of this, and the 2026 Budget raised the tax on F&O trades. This is the reason F&O stays learn-only in this course.

    Part 3

    Worked examples

    Example 1: Who handles your trade?

    You buy 5 shares of HDFC Bank on Groww at 10:30 am on Monday. Name everyone involved.

    1. Groww (broker) checks you have the money and sends the order to NSE.
    2. NSE (exchange) matches it with a seller's order in a fraction of a second.
    3. NSE Clearing becomes the buyer to the seller and the seller to you, and settles the trade.
    4. CDSL (Groww's depository) credits the 5 shares to your demat account on Tuesday.
    5. SEBI set the rules each of them followed.

    Example 2: Is this adviser allowed?

    A Telegram channel charges ₹4,999 a month for "sure-shot" stock tips and shows no registration. What should you do?

    Answer: don't pay. Giving paid stock recommendations needs SEBI registration as a research analyst or investment adviser. Search the name on sebi.gov.in under "Recognised intermediaries". No legitimate adviser promises "sure-shot" returns.

    Example 3: Exchange arbitrage, and why it rarely pays

    On 29 October, Reliance closed ₹1.35 higher on BSE than on NSE. Could you buy 100 on NSE and sell 100 on BSE for ₹135 profit?

    gross gap = 100 × 1.35 = ₹135 STT alone (0.1% × 2 sides on ~₹1,16,800) ≈ ₹234 result ≈ a loss, before other charges

    Answer: no. The gap is smaller than the costs. Professional traders with very low costs close these gaps in milliseconds, which is why prices stay together.

    Example 4: Your share of SEBI's study

    If 93 out of every 100 F&O traders lost money, and you start trading F&O with no edge, what does a fair coin toss look like by comparison?

    Answer: a coin gives you a 50% chance. F&O retail traders, as a group, did far worse than a coin: only about 7 in 100 made money. Costs, leverage and competing with professionals push the odds against beginners.

    Part 5

    Practice task: check the people you deal with

    About 20 minutes.

    • On sebi.gov.in, open "Intermediaries / Market Infrastructure Institutions", then "Recognised intermediaries". Find the SEBI registration number of the broker you plan to use.
    • Search for one finfluencer you follow. Are they registered as an investment adviser or research analyst? Write down what you found.
    • Open the SCORES portal (scores.sebi.gov.in) and read how a complaint is filed. You don't need to file one.
    • On nseindia.com, find today's price of Reliance. On bseindia.com, find it too. Write down the difference.
    • Read the one-page summary of SEBI's F&O study (search "SEBI study F&O traders 2024") and note the one number that stuck with you most.
