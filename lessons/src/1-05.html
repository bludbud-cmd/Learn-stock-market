Indices: Nifty 50, Sensex, Bank Nifty and sector indices

When the news says "the market fell 1% today", it means an index fell 1%. An index is one number that sums up a basket of shares. As a trader you'll check the Nifty before every trade, because most stocks move with it.

Part 1

The idea in plain words

There are over 2,000 companies trading on NSE. Nobody can watch all of them. So exchanges pick a basket of important shares and combine their prices into one number, an index. If most of the basket goes up, the index goes up. It's like the Sensex is the "temperature" of the market: one reading that tells you if things are hot or cold.

How the number is calculated

Big companies count for more. Both the Nifty and Sensex are weighted by free-float market cap: the market value of the shares that are actually available to trade (shares locked up with promoters or the government are left out). So a 2% move in HDFC Bank or Reliance moves the Nifty far more than a 2% move in a smaller member. The few biggest members each make up roughly 8% to 13% of the Nifty.

The index doesn't care about the share price itself, only the company's weight. That's why a stock split or bonus doesn't change the index.

Points vs percent

News says "Nifty fell 230 points". Points alone mean little. 230 points is 1% when the Nifty is at 23,000, but was 4.6% when the Nifty was at 5,000 in 2009. Always convert to percent.

percent change = points change ÷ previous close × 100

Why traders watch indices

  • Direction: on a day the Nifty rises strongly, most stocks rise with it, and on a day it falls hard, most fall. Buying a stock when the whole market is falling is swimming against the tide.
  • Sector strength: comparing sector indices shows where money is flowing. A swing trader prefers stocks from the strongest sectors.
  • Benchmark: if your trading makes 8% in a year when the Nifty made 20%, you'd have done better buying an index fund.
Remember

You can't buy "the Nifty" as a share, but you can buy a Nifty index fund or ETF that holds all 50 stocks in the same weights. It's the simplest long-term investment there is, and the benchmark every trader is measured against.

Worked examples

Example 1: Points to percent

The Nifty closed at 24,055.80 on 1 September 2026 and at 23,053.10 on 30 October 2026. How many points and what percent did it fall?

points = 23,053.10 − 24,055.80 = −1,002.70 percent = −1,002.70 ÷ 24,055.80 × 100 = −4.17%

Answer: about 1,000 points, or 4.2%. A 1,000-point fall sounds scary; 4.2% over two months is a normal pullback.

Example 2: How weights move an index

A tiny index has three stocks with weights 50%, 30% and 20%. In a day they move +2%, −1% and +4%. How much does the index move?

0.50 × (+2%) + 0.30 × (−1%) + 0.20 × (+4%) = +1.0% − 0.3% + 0.8% = +1.5%

Answer: +1.5%. The biggest stock contributes most. On the Nifty, two or three heavyweights moving sharply can push the whole index while most other stocks barely move.

Example 3: Was your trading worth it?

In 2023 you made 12% trading. The Nifty rose 20% that year. How did you do?

Answer: you underperformed the benchmark by 8 percentage points, with much more effort and risk. In a strong year a trader needs to beat a simple index fund to justify the time. In a weak year, like 2026 so far, holding cash and waiting can be a win.
