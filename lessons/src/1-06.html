Brokers, demat and trading accounts: what happens when you press Buy

To trade you need three accounts that work together: a bank account, a trading account and a demat account. This lesson explains each one, follows a real order from your phone to your demat account, and shows how to choose a broker.

About 45 minutesReal data: Reliance trade on NSE, demat account growth 2020–2025, Karvy 2019
Part 1

The idea in plain words

Think of how you'd buy gold coins and keep them in a bank locker. You need money in your bank (to pay), a jeweller (to buy from) and a locker (to keep the coins safe in your name). Shares work the same way, just electronically:

AccountLikeWhat it doesWho runs it
Bank accountYour walletSends money to your broker and receives sale proceedsYour bank
Trading accountThe jeweller's counterLets you place buy and sell orders on NSE and BSEYour broker
Demat accountThe lockerHolds your shares electronically, in your nameA depository (CDSL or NSDL) through a DP (Depository Participant), usually your broker

Brokers open the trading and demat accounts together in one online application. Your demat account has a 16-character ID, called a BO ID or client ID. If your broker shut down tomorrow, your shares would still be safe in your demat account at CDSL or NSDL, and you could move them to another broker.

What you need to open an account

  • PAN card (compulsory for any share trading)
  • Aadhaar linked to your mobile number, for e-KYC and e-signing
  • Bank account proof (a cancelled cheque or bank statement)
  • A short video or selfie check (in-person verification, IPV), done in the app
  • A nominee: who gets your shares if something happens to you. Add one, or formally opt out.

Opening is fully online and usually takes one to three days. Most brokers open accounts free. Some charge a yearly demat AMC (annual maintenance charge).

Full-service vs discount brokers

Full-service brokers (ICICI Securities, HDFC Securities, Kotak Securities) offer research, advice and branches, and charge a percentage of each trade. Discount brokers (Zerodha, Groww, Upstox, Angel One, Dhan) offer an app, low fixed fees and no advice. For a self-taught trader, a discount broker is usually the better fit.

How to choose a broker

  1. Charges: delivery brokerage, intraday brokerage, DP charge on each sale, and AMC. Read the official "charges" page, not an ad. For example, Zerodha's own page (checked November 2026) lists ₹0 for delivery, ₹20 or 0.03% per intraday order (whichever is lower), and ₹15.34 DP charge per stock sold.
  2. Reliability: does the app stay up on busy days? Search "[broker] outage" for recent years.
  3. Tools you'll need later: good charts, GTT orders (lesson 1.08), and a trading API. From Level 4 this course uses a free broker API (Upstox or Angel One) for live paper trading, so one of those is a sensible first choice.
  4. Support and history: how they handle complaints. Check their SEBI registration and any SEBI orders against them.
Never do this

Never share your login, password, TPIN or OTP with anyone, including "relationship managers" or "account handlers" who offer to trade for you. Letting someone else trade your account is the most common way beginners lose money to fraud.

Part 2

What happens when you press Buy

A real example: buying 10 Reliance shares with a limit order at ₹1,165 on Thursday 29 October 2026. Reliance traded between ₹1,155.60 and ₹1,182.10 that day (real NSE data).

  1. 9:47:12 am · Your phoneYou choose Reliance, quantity 10, limit price ₹1,165, product CNC (delivery), and tap Buy.
  2. + a few milliseconds · Broker's risk checkThe broker's system checks you have ₹11,650 plus charges available, that the price is inside the day's allowed range, and that the order is valid. If not, it is rejected here.
  3. + a few milliseconds · NSE order bookThe order joins NSE's order book for Reliance. If a seller is waiting at ₹1,165 or lower, the exchange matches you instantly. If not, your order waits in line by price, then by time.
  4. Matched · Trade confirmationNSE sends a trade confirmation with a unique trade number. Your app shows "Executed". The money is blocked in your trading account.
  5. Evening · Contract noteThe broker emails a contract note listing the price, quantity, brokerage, STT, stamp duty and every other charge. Keep these: they're your tax records.
  6. Friday (T+1) · SettlementNSE Clearing collects ₹11,650 from your broker and pays the seller's broker. The 10 shares move from the seller's demat account to yours at CDSL or NSDL.
  7. Friday evening · Shares in your dematThe shares appear in your holdings. You're now an owner, entitled to any dividend whose record date falls after this.

Selling works in reverse, with one extra step: you authorise the shares to leave your demat account, either with a one-time e-DIS/TPIN OTP from CDSL or NSDL, or with a standing DDPI authorisation you signed with your broker. This rule exists because of the Karvy case in lesson 1.04.

Part 3

Worked examples

Example 1: Money needed

You want 10 Reliance shares at ₹1,165 for delivery. Your trading account has ₹11,500. Will the order go through?

needed = 10 × 1,165 = ₹11,650 (plus about ₹13 of charges) have = ₹11,500 short = ₹150+

Answer: no. The broker's risk check rejects it with "insufficient funds". For delivery (CNC) you must have the full amount. Add money by UPI first, which usually shows up within minutes.

Example 2: Broker shut down

Your broker suddenly stops working. You hold 20 TCS shares and ₹5,000 unused cash with them. What is safe?

Answer: the 20 TCS shares are in your demat account at the depository, in your name, so they can be moved to a new broker. The cash is held by the broker, which is why SEBI requires brokers to return unused client funds to your bank regularly. Don't leave large idle balances in a trading account.

Example 3: The yearly cost of a broker

You plan 50 swing trades a year (50 buys and 50 sells) and, on days off, 30 intraday trades a month of ₹40,000 each. Broker A charges ₹0 for delivery and "₹20 or 0.03% per order, whichever is lower" for intraday. What is the brokerage for a year?

delivery : 100 orders × ₹0 = ₹0 intraday : 0.03% of ₹40,000 = ₹12 (lower than ₹20) → ₹12 per order 30 trades × 2 orders × 12 months = 720 orders 720 × ₹12 = ₹8,640 DP charge: 50 delivery sales × ₹15.34 ≈ ₹767

Answer: about ₹9,400 a year, almost all from intraday. Small per trade, large over a year. Lesson 1.10 adds taxes and the other charges.

Practice task: compare two brokers

About 25 minutes. Don't open an account yet unless you want to; paper trading comes first.

  • Open the official charges pages of Upstox and Angel One (and your current broker, if you have one).
  • Fill in the table: delivery brokerage, intraday brokerage, DP charge per sale, AMC.
  • Check each broker offers GTT orders and a free trading API for retail users.
  • Look up each broker's SEBI registration number on sebi.gov.in.
  • Write down which one you'd pick and one reason why. If you already have a demat account, find your 16-character BO ID in the app.
