What a share is: owning a slice of Reliance or TCS

A share is a small piece of ownership in a real company. When you buy one TCS share, you become one of TCS's owners, with a claim on its profits. This lesson explains what you actually own, how you get paid, and why the price moves.

Part 1

The idea in plain words

Imagine your friend opens a bakery that needs ₹10 lakh. She splits the ownership into 1,000 equal pieces worth ₹1,000 each and you buy 100 of them. You now own 10% of the bakery. If the bakery earns ₹2 lakh profit this year, 10% of that profit belongs to you. If someone later offers ₹15 lakh for the whole bakery, your 10% is worth ₹1.5 lakh.

Each of those pieces is a share. Ownership through shares is called equity, which is why you'll hear "equity market" and "stock market" used for the same thing. A big company like Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) works exactly like the bakery, just with about 362 crore shares instead of 1,000.

What owning a share gives you

  • A share of profits. The company may pay part of its profit to owners as a dividend, a fixed rupee amount per share. The rest is kept to grow the business.
  • A share of the company's value. If the business becomes more valuable, each share does too, and you can sell it to someone else for more than you paid.
  • A vote. Shareholders vote on big decisions such as electing directors, usually online, at the Annual General Meeting (AGM).
  • Limited risk. If the company fails, you can lose what you paid for the shares, but nobody can ask you to pay the company's debts. Your loss is limited to your investment.

Owners vs lenders

A company raises money in two ways. It can borrow (loans, bonds), and lenders get fixed interest whether the company does well or badly. Or it can sell ownership (shares), and owners get whatever is left after everyone else is paid. Owners earn the most when things go well and lose first when things go badly. That's the risk and the reward of shares.

Why the share price moves

A share's price on the exchange is simply the last price at which a buyer and a seller agreed to trade. It moves every second because people keep changing their minds about what the company will earn in the future. Good results, a new product or a falling interest rate can make buyers willing to pay more. Bad news makes sellers willing to accept less. Over years, the price tends to follow the company's profits. Over days and weeks, which is where you'll trade, it follows news, emotion and the balance of buyers and sellers.

Market capitalisation

The value of the whole company, as the market sees it, is called market capitalisation or market cap:

market cap = share price × number of shares

Indian market caps are usually given in crore (1 crore = ₹1,00,00,000) or lakh crore (1 lakh crore = ₹1,00,000 crore).

Remember

A share is not a lottery ticket. It is a slice of a business. The price can swing wildly in the short term, but in the long run it follows how much that business earns.

Part 3

Worked examples

Example 1: How much of TCS do you own?

TCS has about 361.8 crore shares. You buy 10 shares at ₹2,502.60 (the close on 30 Oct 2026). What do you pay, what is TCS's market cap, and what fraction do you own?

you pay = 10 × 2,502.60 = ₹25,026 market cap = 2,502.60 × 361.8 crore ≈ ₹9,05,440 crore ≈ ₹9.05 lakh crore your share = 10 ÷ 3,61,80,00,000 ≈ 0.0000003%

Answer: ₹25,026 makes you a real but tiny owner. Your vote barely counts, but your claim on profits is exactly proportional to the shares you hold.

Example 2: Dividend income

In calendar year 2025 TCS paid dividends of ₹76 (January, including a ₹66 special dividend), ₹30 (June), ₹11 (July) and ₹11 (October) per share. What did 10 shares earn, and what is the yield at ₹2,502.60?

dividend per share = 76 + 30 + 11 + 11 = ₹128 for 10 shares = ₹1,280 dividend yield = 128 ÷ 2,502.60 = 5.1%

Answer: ₹1,280 for the year. Special dividends don't repeat every year, so a normal yield is lower. Dividends are added to your income and taxed at your slab rate.

Example 3: Bonus maths

You held 15 Reliance shares before the 1:1 bonus. The price was ₹2,655.70. What do you hold after, and at what price would the value be unchanged?

shares after = 15 + 15 = 30 value before = 15 × 2,655.70 = ₹39,835.50 break-even = 39,835.50 ÷ 30 = ₹1,327.85

Answer: 30 shares. Any price above ₹1,327.85 means you're ahead. The stock opened at ₹1,337, so holders were very slightly better off, not 50% poorer.

Example 4: Price vs value

Share A costs ₹100 and share B costs ₹2,500. Which is "cheaper"?

Answer: you can't tell from the price. A ₹100 share of a company with 1,000 crore shares has a ₹1 lakh crore market cap. What matters is the price compared with the company's profits, which you learn in Level 2 (the P/E ratio). Never buy a stock just because its price looks low.
