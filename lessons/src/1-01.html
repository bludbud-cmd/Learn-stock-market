Part 1

Start from zero: the words everyone uses

No knowledge needed. Read this slowly; every later lesson builds on these few words.

1. What is a company?

A company is a business that is registered with the government as its own "person" in law. It can own things, borrow money, sign contracts and earn profit in its own name. Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), Reliance Industries and HDFC Bank are companies. So is a small local firm that makes biscuits.

Every company is owned by someone. When a company starts, its founders own all of it.

2. Why would owners split their company into pieces?

Imagine your friend Priya starts a bakery. It's doing well and she wants to open five more branches, which needs ₹50 lakh. She doesn't have that much. She could borrow from a bank and pay interest, or she could invite other people to become part-owners: they give her money now, and in return they own part of the bakery and share in its future profits.

To make this fair and simple, she divides the ownership of the bakery into many equal pieces.

3. What is a share?

Each equal piece of ownership is called a share. Think of the company as a big pizza cut into 1,000 equal slices. Each slice is one share. If you own 10 slices, you own 10 out of 1,000, which is 1% of the pizza: 1% of the business.

Example

Priya's bakery is split into 1,000 shares. You buy 50 shares. You now own 50 ÷ 1,000 = 5% of the bakery. If the bakery makes ₹2,00,000 profit in a year, 5% of that profit, ₹10,000, is the part that belongs to you as an owner.

4. Share, stock, equity: are they different?

In everyday talk in India, share and stock mean the same thing: a piece of ownership in a company. "I bought Infosys shares" and "I bought Infosys stock" mean the same. Equity is the formal word for ownership, so you'll also hear "equity shares" or "equity market". In this course we'll mostly say "share".

5. What is a shareholder, and shareholding?

A person who owns shares is a shareholder (also called a stockholder). The shares you own, taken together, are your shareholding or your "holdings". If you own 10 shares of TCS and 5 shares of Reliance, your shareholding is those 15 shares in two companies. Your broker's app has a screen called "Holdings" that shows exactly this.

6. What does a shareholder get?

  • A part of the profits. Companies sometimes pay owners a share of profit in cash, called a dividend (for example ₹10 for every share you own).
  • A part of the growth. If the company grows and becomes more valuable, each share becomes worth more, and you can sell it for more than you paid.
  • A small vote on big decisions, in proportion to how many shares you hold.
  • The risk, too. If the company does badly, your shares lose value. If it fails completely, you can lose all the money you paid for them, but never more than that.

7. What is the stock market?

Once a company has many shareholders, some of them will want to sell their shares and other people will want to buy them. They need a place to meet. That place is the stock market (or share market).

Think of a vegetable mandi. Farmers bring tomatoes, buyers come to buy, and the price goes up when many people want tomatoes and down when there are too many. The stock market is a mandi for shares, except it's fully electronic: buyers and sellers place orders on a phone app, and a computer matches them in a fraction of a second.

India has two big stock exchanges where this happens: the NSE (National Stock Exchange) and the BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange), both in Mumbai. Only companies that have been approved and "listed" there can be traded. You'll learn how a company gets listed in lesson 1.03.

8. Why do share prices go up and down?

The price of a share is simply the last price at which a buyer and a seller agreed to trade. If more people want to buy a share than sell it, buyers offer higher prices and the price rises. If more want to sell, the price falls. People's wish to buy or sell changes with news: good profits, a new product, a bad result, a scandal, or the mood of the whole market.

10. One more tool: percent (%)

Almost everything in the market is measured in percent. "Percent" means "out of 100". 5% means 5 out of every 100.

5% of ₹2,000 = 2,000 × 5 ÷ 100 = ₹100 a price going from ₹200 to ₹210: change = 210 − 200 = ₹10 percent = 10 ÷ 200 × 100 = 5% rise

If this feels new, don't worry. You'll use it so often that it becomes automatic.

The whole of Part 1 in four lines

A company is a business. Its ownership is cut into equal pieces called shares (or stocks). People who own shares are shareholders, and what they own is their shareholding. The stock market (NSE and BSE) is where shares are bought and sold, at prices that move with demand.

Part 2

Why your money needs to grow

Think of a plate of idli at your favourite canteen. If it cost ₹30 ten years ago and costs ₹50 today, your ₹100 note now buys fewer plates. The note didn't change. Prices did. This slow, steady rise in prices is called inflation.

In India, the government measures inflation with the Consumer Price Index (CPI). It tracks the price of a fixed basket of things households buy: food, fuel, rent, clothes, school fees, medicine. If the CPI goes up 5% in a year, the same basket costs 5% more than it did a year ago.

So every rupee you keep has a hidden enemy. If your money earns less than inflation, you are getting poorer even though the number in your account goes up. The number that matters is your real return:

real return ≈ what your money earned − inflation

A savings account paying 3% while inflation runs at 5% gives a real return of about −2% a year. You lose buying power slowly and quietly.

Where money can go

  • Cash at home: earns 0%. Loses the full rate of inflation every year.
  • Savings account: about 2.5% to 3.5%. Safe and instant, but usually below inflation.
  • Fixed deposit (FD): about 6% to 7.5% at most banks in recent years. Safe and predictable, roughly equal to inflation after tax.
  • Gold: no income, but historically has kept up with or beaten inflation over long periods, with big swings in between.
  • Shares (the stock market): you own part of real businesses. Over long periods Indian shares have beaten inflation by a wide margin, but they can fall 30% to 60% along the way.

That last line is the whole story of this course. Shares grow faster because they are bumpy. Investors are paid for sitting through the bumps. Traders, which is what you are training to become, try to profit from the bumps themselves. Both need you to understand why the market exists in the first place: companies need money to grow, and people with savings want those savings to grow.

Compounding: growth on top of growth

When your money grows, next year's growth is on the bigger amount. ₹1,00,000 growing 10% becomes ₹1,10,000, then ₹1,21,000, then ₹1,33,100. That extra ₹1,000 and ₹2,100 is growth on growth. It is called compounding, and over 20 years it does most of the work.

future value = today's amount × (1 + yearly rate) ^ years

A quick shortcut is the Rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly rate to see roughly how many years it takes to double. At 6% money doubles in about 12 years. At 12% it doubles in about 6.

Remember

Inflation is a tax on doing nothing. Investing is not about getting rich quickly. Its first job is to stop your savings from shrinking.

Part 4

Worked examples

Example 1: ₹100 in 10 years

If inflation averages 5% a year, what will ₹100 buy in 10 years, in today's money?

  1. Prices after 10 years = 1.05 ^ 10 = 1.629 times today's prices.
  2. So ₹100 then buys what ₹100 ÷ 1.629 buys today.
100 ÷ 1.05^10 = 100 ÷ 1.629 = ₹61.39

Answer: about ₹61. Your ₹100 note loses almost 40% of its buying power in 10 years. India's real CPI from March 2015 to March 2025 rose 1.62 times, so this is exactly what happened.

Example 2: FD vs inflation

You put ₹2,00,000 in an FD at 7% for 5 years. Inflation averages 5.5%. What is your real gain?

  1. FD value = 2,00,000 × 1.07^5 = 2,00,000 × 1.4026 = ₹2,80,510.
  2. Prices rise 1.055^5 = 1.3070 times.
  3. In today's money, 2,80,510 ÷ 1.3070 = ₹2,14,622.

Answer: the account shows ₹80,510 of interest, but your real gain is only about ₹14,600. And FD interest is taxed at your income slab, which can wipe out most of that.

Example 3: Rule of 72 with real Sensex numbers

The Sensex grew about 10.5% a year from 2000 to 2026. Roughly how often did it double?

72 ÷ 10.5 ≈ 6.9 years per doubling 26.8 years ÷ 6.9 ≈ 3.9 doublings 2^3.9 ≈ 14.9 times

Answer: about every 7 years. 5,205 × 14.3 = 74,460, which matches the real chart closely. The rule works.

Example 4: The price of the bumps

The Nifty 50 closed at 6,287.85 on 8 January 2008 and at 2,524.20 on 27 October 2008. How much did it fall, and what gain was needed to get back?

fall = 2,524.20 ÷ 6,287.85 − 1 = −59.9% gain needed = 6,287.85 ÷ 2,524.20 − 1 = +149%

Answer: a 60% fall needs a 149% rise to recover. The Nifty only closed above its January 2008 level again in late 2010. This maths of losses comes back in Level 5, because it's why traders cut losing trades early.
