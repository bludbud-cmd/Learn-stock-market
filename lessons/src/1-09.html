Level 1 · Market basics · Lesson 1.09

Settlement, circuits, bid and ask, volume and liquidity

This lesson covers the plumbing of every trade: when the money and shares actually move, the limits on how far a price can move in a day, and how to tell whether you'll be able to get in and out of a stock at a fair price.

Part 1

The ideas in plain words

Settlement: T+1

When you buy, the trade is agreed instantly, but the money and shares change hands later. This is settlement. India settles all stock trades on T+1: one working day after the trade day (T). India finished moving every stock to T+1 on 27 January 2023, earlier than most big markets. Since March 2024 an optional same-day (T+0) settlement also exists for a limited list of stocks, but T+1 is the normal case.

  • Buy on Monday: shares reach your demat on Tuesday. Weekends and holidays don't count, so a Friday buy settles on Monday.
  • Sell on Monday: the money is credited to your trading account on Tuesday, and you can withdraw it to your bank after that.
  • Selling something you don't own (for example a CNC sell with no shares in your demat) causes a short delivery. The exchange buys the shares in an auction and charges you the difference plus a penalty. It can be very expensive.

Bid, ask and spread

At any moment there are buyers waiting and sellers waiting. The highest price a buyer is offering is the bid. The lowest price a seller will accept is the ask (or offer). The gap between them is the spread. Your broker app shows them in the market depth: the top 5 bids and asks with the quantity waiting at each.

If you place a market buy, you pay the ask. A market sell gets the bid. So every round trip with market orders costs you the spread. In Reliance it's usually 5 or 10 paise on a ₹1,165 share. In a small, thinly traded stock it can be 1% or more.

Volume and liquidity

Volume is how many shares traded in a period. Liquidity is how easily you can buy or sell a meaningful amount without moving the price. High volume, a tight spread and deep market depth mean good liquidity. For trading, compare rupee turnover (volume × price), not just share volume: a ₹15 stock can trade 10 crore shares and still be far less liquid than a ₹1,165 stock trading 1.2 crore.

Circuits: limits on how far a price can move

  • Stock price bands: most stocks may move only 2%, 5%, 10% or 20% from the previous close in one day, as set by the exchange. At the limit the stock is "in upper circuit" (only buyers left, no sellers) or "lower circuit" (only sellers). You may not be able to sell at all until the next day, or longer.
  • F&O stocks (large, liquid stocks like Reliance) have no fixed band but a dynamic band that the exchange widens step by step during big moves.
  • Market-wide circuit breakers: if the Nifty or Sensex falls (or rises) 10%, 15% or 20% in a day, trading in the whole market halts. A 10% move before 1 pm stops trading for 45 minutes. A 20% move ends trading for the day.
Watch out

Small, cheap stocks with 5% bands can hit lower circuit for days in a row. If you're stuck holding one, you can't sell however much you want to. As a swing trader you'll stick to liquid stocks (Nifty 200 and similar) for exactly this reason.

Part 3

Worked examples

Example 1: Settlement dates

You buy on Friday 30 October 2026 and sell on Monday 2 November. When do the shares arrive and when do you get the money?

  1. Friday's buy settles on the next working day: Monday 2 November. The shares reach your demat on Monday.
  2. Monday's sale settles on Tuesday 3 November. The money is credited on Tuesday.

Answer: shares Monday, money Tuesday. If you had sold on Friday itself, before the shares were in your demat, it would be a same-day intraday trade (which needs MIS or is treated as intraday for charges and tax).

Example 2: The cost of the spread

A small stock shows bid ₹198.00 and ask ₹200.00. You market-buy 100 shares and immediately market-sell them. What did you lose?

buy at ask = 100 × 200.00 = ₹20,000 sell at bid = 100 × 198.00 = ₹19,800 lost to the spread = ₹200 (1% of the trade, before any charges)

Answer: ₹200 just for crossing the spread. The same round trip in Reliance with a 10 paise spread costs about ₹10 on 100 shares. Use limit orders in wide-spread stocks, or avoid them.

Example 3: Circuit limits

A stock with a 5% price band closed at ₹240 yesterday. What are today's upper and lower circuit prices?

upper = 240 × 1.05 = ₹252.00 lower = 240 × 0.95 = ₹228.00

Answer: ₹252 and ₹228. If it's stuck at ₹228 with only sellers, your sell order waits in a queue and may not fill today.

Example 4: The market-wide halt

The Nifty closed at 8,745.45 on 20 March 2020. At what level does a 10% market-wide circuit trigger?

8,745.45 × 0.90 = 7,870.9

Answer: about 7,871. On 23 March the Nifty fell through it, trading halted for 45 minutes, then continued falling to a low of 7,583.60.

Part 5

Practice task: read market depth

About 15 minutes during market hours (a day off or a lunch break works).

  • Open the market depth for Reliance in your broker's app or on nseindia.com. Write down the best bid, best ask and spread.
  • Do the same for one small cap stock outside the Nifty 500 (any you've heard of). Compare the spreads in % (spread ÷ ask × 100).
  • On nseindia.com, find each stock's price band (shown as "Price band" or "Upper/lower band").
  • Note each stock's volume so far today and multiply by price to get rupee turnover.
  • Write one rule for your stock list: for example, "I only trade stocks with at least ₹50 crore turnover a day and a spread under 0.1%."
