How a company lists: IPOs, primary and secondary market

A company first sells shares to the public in an IPO. After that, the shares change hands between investors every day on NSE and BSE. This lesson follows four real Indian IPOs, from application to their first year of trading.

About 55 minutesReal data: Zomato 2021, Paytm 2021, LIC 2022, IRCTC 2019
Part 1

The idea in plain words

Most companies start as private businesses owned by founders and early investors. When a company wants a lot of money to grow, or its early investors want to sell some of their stake, it can offer shares to the public for the first time. This is an IPO, an Initial Public Offering. After the IPO, its shares are listed on the stock exchange and anyone with a demat account can buy or sell them.

Two markets

Almost all trading, and everything you'll do as a swing trader, happens in the secondary market. The primary market is how shares get there in the first place.

How an IPO works, step by step

  1. Draft papers. The company files a DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) with SEBI. It describes the business, its finances, risks and how the money will be used. Anyone can read it on SEBI's website.
  2. SEBI review. SEBI checks the disclosures. It doesn't judge whether the company is a good investment, only that the facts are told.
  3. Price band and lot size. The company announces a price band (for example ₹72 to ₹76) and a lot, the minimum number of shares you can apply for.
  4. Bidding (usually 3 working days). You apply through your broker's app using UPI. The money is only blocked in your bank account (this system is called ASBA), not taken.
  5. Allotment. If more people apply than there are shares, it's oversubscribed. Retail investors then get shares by lottery: usually one lot each for the lucky ones.
  6. Listing. Since December 2023 SEBI requires listing 3 working days after the issue closes (T+3). On listing day, shares start trading and the price is set by the market. The gap between the IPO price and the first price is the listing gain (or loss).

Fresh issue vs offer for sale

In a fresh issue the company creates new shares and gets the money to grow. In an offer for sale (OFS) existing owners sell their shares and the money goes to them, not the company. Paytm's ₹18,300 crore IPO in 2021 was ₹8,300 crore fresh issue and ₹10,000 crore OFS. LIC's IPO in 2022 was entirely an OFS by the Government of India. Always check the split in the prospectus: a big OFS means insiders are cashing out.

Who gets how many shares

Every IPO reserves parts for qualified institutional buyers (QIBs: mutual funds, banks, insurers), non-institutional investors (wealthy individuals, NIIs) and retail investors (applications up to ₹2 lakh). For a profitable company at least 35% goes to retail. For a loss-making company like Paytm, only 10% did.

Watch out

"Grey market premium" (GMP) numbers on social media are unofficial, unregulated and often wrong. They are a guess, not a price. Base your view on the prospectus, not on chat groups. A hot IPO is not a safe one.

Four real IPOs and their first year

The pattern

Of these four, the two that did well were small, profitable, reasonably priced issues. The two that did badly were giant issues priced at the top, where the main sellers were existing owners. Listing-day excitement told you very little about the next year.

Part 3

Worked examples

Example 1: Applying for Zomato

The lot was 195 shares at the upper band of ₹76. How much was blocked for one lot, and what was the listing-day gain if you sold at the ₹126 close?

blocked = 195 × 76 = ₹14,820 sold at = 195 × 126 = ₹24,570 gain = 24,570 − 14,820 = ₹9,750 (+65.8%, before charges and tax)

Answer: ₹9,750 on one lot. But with 38 times oversubscription, most retail applicants got nothing, and their blocked money was simply released.

Example 2: Paytm's listing day

One lot of 6 shares at ₹2,150. What was the loss at the day-one close of ₹1,560.80?

paid = 6 × 2,150 = ₹12,900 worth = 6 × 1,560.80 = ₹9,364.80 loss = ₹3,535.20 (−27.4%)

Answer: more than a quarter of the money gone on the first day. If you'd held one year, 6 × 481.70 = ₹2,890, a loss of ₹10,010.

Example 3: LIC with the retail discount

A retail investor paid ₹904 a share for one lot of 15. Was the listing a gain or a loss at the ₹872 open?

paid = 15 × 904 = ₹13,560 open = 15 × 872 = ₹13,080 loss = ₹480 (−3.5%)

Answer: a small loss even after the discount. The discount reduced the damage but didn't prevent it.

Example 4: Reading oversubscription

An IPO's retail portion is subscribed 50 times. About what are your chances of getting one lot?

Answer: roughly 1 in 50 or worse, because each successful applicant usually gets only one lot. Applying from several family members' accounts (each with its own PAN) is allowed. Applying twice from one PAN is not, and both applications get rejected.

Part 5

Practice task: track a live IPO

About 20 minutes now, plus a few minutes on its listing day.

  • Open the "Upcoming issues" or "Current issues" page on nseindia.com (under Market Data, IPO) and pick one mainboard IPO (not SME).
  • Write down: price band, lot size, issue size, and how much is fresh issue vs offer for sale.
  • Open its RHP and find one risk the company itself lists in the "Risk factors" section.
  • On the last day, note the retail and QIB subscription figures from the NSE page.
  • On listing day, note the open price and close, and calculate the listing gain or loss for one lot with the calculator above. Don't apply with real money yet; this is observation only.
